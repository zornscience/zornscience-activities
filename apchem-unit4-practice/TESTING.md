Original aligned practice for College Board CED topics 4.1 to 4.9. These are not official exam questions. The following solutions were established before writing the prompts, choices and translations. All atomic masses in g/mol match Units 1 to 3: H 1.01, C 12.01, N 14.01, O 16.00, Na 22.99, Mg 24.31, Al 26.98, S 32.06, Cl 35.45, K 39.10, Ca 40.08, Fe 55.85. Particle pictures are models, with solvent omitted where stated.

4.1-1 | A new insoluble solid after mixing clear solutions indicates a new substance. Evaporation and bubbles alone are ambiguous. | Formation of a precipitate
4.1-2 | Heating water produces water vapor with the same molecular identity. | Boiling alone does not establish a chemical reaction
4.1-3 | A sealed system retains all atoms and therefore its total mass. | Total mass stays constant
4.1-4 | A balanced reaction counts atoms of each element, not necessarily molecules. | Atoms of each element are conserved
4.1-5 | A temperature rise without external heating supports energy release; identity evidence strengthens the conclusion. | Exothermic reaction evidence
4.1-6 | 2Mg(s) + O<sub>2</sub>(g) → 2MgO(s). 0.300 mol Mg × 1/2 mol O<sub>2</sub> per mol Mg. | 0.150 mol O<sub>2</sub>
4.2-1 | CaCl<sub>2</sub> and Na<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> dissociate; CaCO<sub>3</sub> is insoluble. Cancel Na<sup>+</sup> and Cl<sup>−</sup>. | Ca<sup>2+</sup>(aq) + CO<sub>3</sub><sup>2−</sup>(aq) → CaCO<sub>3</sub>(s)
4.2-2 | For BaCl<sub>2</sub> + Na<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>, BaSO<sub>4</sub> precipitates; Na<sup>+</sup> and Cl<sup>−</sup> remain unchanged. | Na<sup>+</sup> and Cl<sup>−</sup> are spectator ions
4.2-3 | A complete ionic equation separates soluble strong electrolytes but retains solid and liquid species. | Dissociate aqueous salts, retain precipitate
4.2-4 | Weak CH<sub>3</sub>COOH stays molecular; OH<sup>−</sup> removes a proton and water forms. | CH<sub>3</sub>COOH(aq) + OH<sup>−</sup>(aq) → CH<sub>3</sub>COO<sup>−</sup>(aq) + H<sub>2</sub>O(l)
4.2-5 | All ions remain aqueous for NaCl and KNO<sub>3</sub>; complete ionic equation cancels entirely. | No net ionic reaction
4.2-6 | CaCl<sub>2</sub>(aq) + Na<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub>(aq) → CaCO<sub>3</sub>(s) + 2NaCl(aq). Per one formula unit of each reactant, 2 Na<sup>+</sup> and 2 Cl<sup>−</sup> do not react. | 4 spectator ions, exact
4.3-1 | 2H<sub>2</sub>(g) + O<sub>2</sub>(g) → 2H<sub>2</sub>O(g); three O<sub>2</sub> would require six H<sub>2</sub>, but only four exist. Two O<sub>2</sub> react with four H<sub>2</sub>. | 4 H<sub>2</sub>O molecules and 1 O<sub>2</sub> molecule remain
4.3-2 | 2CO(g) + O<sub>2</sub>(g) → 2CO<sub>2</sub>(g); 6 CO consume 3 O<sub>2</sub>, leaving 1 of the initial 4 O<sub>2</sub>. | CO is limiting
4.3-3 | One Ca<sup>2+</sup> combines with one CO<sub>3</sub><sup>2−</sup> in solid; Na<sup>+</sup> and Cl<sup>−</sup> remain separate in solution. | 2 CaCO<sub>3</sub> units, 4 Na<sup>+</sup> and 4 Cl<sup>−</sup> in the stated model
4.3-4 | N<sub>2</sub>(g) + 3H<sub>2</sub>(g) → 2NH<sub>3</sub>(g) conserves 2 N atoms and 6 H atoms. | 1 N<sub>2</sub> plus 3 H<sub>2</sub> gives 2 NH<sub>3</sub>
4.3-5 | 3 N<sub>2</sub> and 6 H<sub>2</sub>: min(3, 6/3) = 2 reaction groups; 2 × 2 NH<sub>3</sub>. | 4 NH<sub>3</sub> molecules, exact
4.3-6 | 5 H<sub>2</sub> + 2 O<sub>2</sub> can consume only 4 H<sub>2</sub> and both O<sub>2</sub>. | 1 H<sub>2</sub> molecule left, exact
4.4-1 | Vaporizing ethanol separates intact molecules; C−C, C−O, C−H and O−H connectivity remains. | Overcome intermolecular attractions
4.4-2 | Burning methane rearranges covalent bonds into CO<sub>2</sub> and H<sub>2</sub>O. | Chemical change with bonds broken and formed
4.4-3 | Crushing ionic salt changes crystal size, without changing composition or forming new substances. | Physical change
4.4-4 | Dissolving sugar disperses intact molecules and changes solute-solvent attractions. | Physical change, covalent framework retained
4.4-5 | Electrolysis forms H<sub>2</sub> and O<sub>2</sub>, distinct substances, requiring O−H bond rearrangement. | Chemical change
4.4-6 | 2H<sub>2</sub>O(l) → 2H<sub>2</sub>(g) + O<sub>2</sub>(g); each initial water molecule has 2 O−H bonds. Count net original bonds replaced, not mechanism events. | 4 O−H bonds, exact
4.5-1 | N<sub>2</sub>(g) + 3H<sub>2</sub>(g) → 2NH<sub>3</sub>(g): coefficient ratio for converting H<sub>2</sub> to NH<sub>3</sub> is 2/3. | 2 mol NH<sub>3</sub> per 3 mol H<sub>2</sub>
4.5-2 | 2Mg(s) + O<sub>2</sub>(g) → 2MgO(s). M(MgO) = 40.31. 4.862/24.31 = 0.2000 mol Mg; × 40.31 = 8.062 g. | 8.062 g MgO, four significant figures
4.5-3 | N<sub>2</sub>(g) + 3H<sub>2</sub>(g) → 2NH<sub>3</sub>(g). 0.250 mol N<sub>2</sub> could give 0.500 mol NH<sub>3</sub>; 0.600 mol H<sub>2</sub> gives 0.400 mol. | 0.400 mol NH<sub>3</sub>, H<sub>2</sub> limiting
4.5-4 | 2Al(s) + 3Cl<sub>2</sub>(g) → 2AlCl<sub>3</sub>(s). Al is limiting with excess Cl<sub>2</sub>. M(AlCl<sub>3</sub>) = 133.33; (2.698/26.98) × 133.33 = 13.333 g. | 13.33 g theoretical yield, four significant figures
4.5-5 | Percent yield = actual/theoretical × 100 = 8.10/9.00 × 100. | 90.0%, three significant figures
4.5-6 | CaCO<sub>3</sub>(s) → CaO(s) + CO<sub>2</sub>(g). M(CaCO<sub>3</sub>) = 100.09, M(CaO) = 56.08. (10.009/100.09) × 56.08 × 0.800 = 4.4864 g. | 4.49 g actual CaO, three significant figures
4.6-1 | HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H<sub>2</sub>O(l). Equivalence is the stoichiometric amount, endpoint is the observed indicator signal. | Equivalence and endpoint are distinct concepts
4.6-2 | A buret measures delivered volume; NaOH of known concentration is titrant, acid sample is analyte. | Buret, titrant and analyte roles
4.6-3 | 0.1000 M NaOH × 0.03000 L = 0.003000 mol OH<sup>−</sup>, equal to HCl at equivalence; divide by 0.02500 L. | 0.1200 M HCl
4.6-4 | H<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>(aq) + 2H<sub>2</sub>O(l). 0.02000 L × 0.1500 M × 2/0.2000 M = 0.03000 L. | 30.00 mL NaOH
4.6-5 | Buret delivery = final minus initial = 24.86 − 1.24. | 23.62 mL, two decimal places
4.6-6 | Overshooting to 25.50 mL instead of 25.00 mL gives (0.1000 × 0.02550)/0.02500 = 0.1020 M. Computed molarity scales with delivered titrant volume. | 0.1020 M, too high compared with true 0.1000 M
4.7-1 | Ca<sup>2+</sup> and CO<sub>3</sub><sup>2−</sup> combine to form insoluble CaCO<sub>3</sub> with no oxidation number changes. | Precipitation reaction
4.7-2 | HCl transfers H<sup>+</sup> to OH<sup>−</sup> to form water; no oxidation numbers change. | Acid-base reaction
4.7-3 | Zn changes 0 to +2 and Cu changes +2 to 0. | Redox reaction
4.7-4 | Carbonate gains protons from acid and forms CO<sub>2</sub> and water; carbon stays +4. | Acid-base gas evolution, not redox
4.7-5 | Burning CH<sub>4</sub> changes C from −4 to +4 and oxygen from 0 to −2. | Combustion is redox
4.7-6 | Excess carbonate precipitates every Ca<sup>2+</sup>; CaCl<sub>2</sub>:CaCO<sub>3</sub> is 1:1. 0.0200 mol × 1. | 0.0200 mol CaCO<sub>3</sub>
4.8-1 | NH<sub>3</sub>(aq) + H<sub>2</sub>O(l) → NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup>(aq) + OH<sup>−</sup>(aq): ammonia accepts H<sup>+</sup> from water. | NH<sub>3</sub> is a Brønsted-Lowry base
4.8-2 | HCl(aq) + H<sub>2</sub>O(l) → H<sub>3</sub>O<sup>+</sup>(aq) + Cl<sup>−</sup>(aq): HCl donates H<sup>+</sup>. | HCl is a Brønsted-Lowry acid, Cl<sup>−</sup> its conjugate base
4.8-3 | A conjugate pair differs by one H<sup>+</sup> with charge differing by one. | NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup>/NH<sub>3</sub> pair
4.8-4 | CH<sub>3</sub>COOH gives H<sup>+</sup> to NH<sub>3</sub>, making CH<sub>3</sub>COO<sup>−</sup> and NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup>. | CH<sub>3</sub>COO<sup>−</sup> is conjugate base, NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup> conjugate acid
4.8-5 | Strong acid ionizes essentially completely; a weak acid ionizes partially at comparable concentration. | Strength describes extent of ionization, not concentration
4.8-6 | HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H<sub>2</sub>O(l) gives 1 mol OH<sup>−</sup> per mol HCl. | 0.0150 mol OH<sup>−</sup> for 0.0150 mol HCl
4.9-1 | Zn(s) + Cu<sup>2+</sup>(aq) → Zn<sup>2+</sup>(aq) + Cu(s): Zn loses two electrons. | Zn oxidized, Cu<sup>2+</sup> reduced
4.9-2 | The species that gains electrons causes oxidation of the other species. | Cu<sup>2+</sup> is oxidizing agent
4.9-3 | Zn supplies electrons and causes Cu<sup>2+</sup> reduction. | Zn is reducing agent
4.9-4 | Fe<sup>3+</sup>(aq) + e<sup>−</sup> → Fe<sup>2+</sup>(aq) balances atoms and charge, with electron on reactant side. | Reduction half-reaction
4.9-5 | SO<sub>4</sub><sup>2−</sup>: x + 4(−2) = −2, so x = +6. | +6 sulfur oxidation number, exact and unitless
4.9-6 | 2Al(s) + 3Cu<sup>2+</sup>(aq) → 2Al<sup>3+</sup>(aq) + 3Cu(s): each Al loses 3 electrons, 2 × 3 = 6. | 6 electrons transferred, exact

For 4.8-1, the equilibrium is shown in the prompt as a forward proton-transfer event using →, without claiming complete ionization. State symbols and true subscript/superscript markup are used in the published questions. The audit, page and data use real subscript and superscript notation.
