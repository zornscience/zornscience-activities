# Teacher notes: Genetics Lab: Crossing Fruit Flies

## What students hand in

The lab has no typed answers and no completion code. At the end, students press Copy my data and paste into your worksheet or document. Each paste starts with the lab seed. The data is tab-separated, so it also pastes into a spreadsheet as columns. It holds:

- Cross 1: the trait and parents they chose, the predicted ratio in lowest terms, the prediction as counts out of 4 and percentages, and the counts and percentages at 8, 40 and 200 offspring.
- F1 × F1: the same for Nn × Nn.
- The three mystery flies: trait, dominant and recessive offspring counts, the student's answer and the real genotype (shown only once they have solved it).
- The optional two-trait cross, if they did it.
- Three conclusion sentence starters.

Open the lab with `?seed=NUMBER` to see exactly what a student saw: the seed is the first line of their pasted data. The same seed always gives the same mystery flies and, for the same choices and the same number of presses of Raise again, the same offspring. The Share link never includes a seed.

## Things to expect in class

- **A sample of 8 can match exactly.** For Nn × Nn, 6 normal out of 8 (a perfect 3 : 1) happens about 31% of the time; for a 1 : 1 cross, 4 of 8 happens about 27% of the time. So a student's 200-fly sample beats their 8-fly sample only about two times in three. Over a whole class, bigger samples are clearly closer (average distance from the prediction over 20,000 seeds: 11.6 percentage points at 8 offspring, 5.4 at 40 and 2.4 at 200 for a 3 : 1 cross; 13.6, 6.2 and 2.8 for a 1 : 1 cross). The lab's feedback tells a student when their own 8 happened to beat their 200 and suggests raising both again. This is a good class discussion: pool everyone's 8s and 200s.
- **A heterozygous mystery fly can look homozygous.** If all test-cross offspring show the dominant trait, homozygous is the best answer, but a heterozygous fly does this 1 time in 1,024 with 10 offspring. If it happens, the student is told their reasoning was right and the fly was heterozygous anyway.
- **Rounding.** Percentages show one decimal place, so two shares add to 100.

## The science, and what it leaves out

- Vestigial wings (*vg*, chromosome 2) and sepia eyes (*se*, chromosome 3) are real autosomal recessive mutations, so they assort independently. White eyes was not used because it is X-linked.
- Every offspring takes one equally likely allele from each parent (two coin flips per gene). There is no linkage, no lethality and no environmental effect. Real vestigial flies raised warm can grow partial wings; the About this model note says so.
- The step 1 parents are true-breeding (NN × nn). The prediction "the vestigial allele is hidden" is the one step 5 supports.
