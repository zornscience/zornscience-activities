Explore the magnet drop

  1. Select an intact copper, slotted copper, or plastic tube.
  2. Press Release magnets. An identical magnet drops freely beside the tube for comparison.
  3. Enable the cutaway to see inside. Reveal eddy currents and induced fields to investigate the braking force.
  4. Pause or use slow motion to inspect the current loops above and below the moving magnet. Reverse the poles and repeat: the currents reverse, but the braking still points upward.

Copper is not ferromagnetic. Changing magnetic flux drives circulating currents; their magnetic fields oppose the change (Lenz’s law). A lengthwise slot interrupts the large current loops.

In a real lab, secure the tube and use a soft catch tray. Strong neodymium magnets can pinch fingers and damage electronics.

How the model works

Faraday’s law: ε = −dΦB/dt. A falling magnet changes magnetic flux through conducting loops in the tube. Above the magnet, the induced field tends to maintain the decreasing flux; below it, the induced field opposes the increasing flux. Both effects produce an upward force on the falling magnet. Gravitational potential energy becomes thermal energy in the copper.

This qualitative model integrates gravity and a speed-dependent magnetic drag, F = −kv, with k proportional to magnet strength squared. Inside the tube, drag can balance weight and produce terminal speed. Slotted copper has much weaker residual braking; plastic and the free-fall comparison have none. Entry and exit effects are smoothed. Air resistance is omitted. Current loops, fields, poles, force arrows, tube wall thickness and magnet size are enlarged or simplified; the cutaway is a visualization and does not physically cut the tube. Strength changes magnetic moment only, with mass fixed. Values illustrate behavior rather than predict a particular commercial magnet.
